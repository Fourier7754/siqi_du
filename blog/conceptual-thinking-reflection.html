A Reflection on Conceptual Thinking

2026-07-26

A STEM background trains you to break things down and solve them one at a time. It works. But watching the Agent space over the past two years, a different pattern kept surfacing: several of the field's bigger breakthroughs were not technical advances. They were syntheses. Someone looked at what everyone was already doing and gave it a name.

MCP took tool use and context sharing—things people had been doing for years—and called it a protocol. Skill looked at prompt layering and workflow structuring, techniques scattered across every codebase, and defined a general spec for packaging and distributing any composable capability. OpenClaw took agent functionality that already worked and labeled it the "self-iterative agent." Its reflection and memory mechanisms were basic. But once the idea of an agent that evolves had a label, it caught. The common thread: when a concept gets defined clearly, the building blocks that were already lying around rearrange into something that moves faster.

Talking with friends in economics and management, a gap kept coming up. When they encounter a phenomenon, their first reflex is "What structure is this? What framework fits?" The engineering reflex is "How do I build it? How do I make it faster?" They were trained to go from the specific to the abstract—to pull concepts and frameworks out of concrete cases. Engineers are trained the other way, from a given concept down into execution.

Looking back at my own work, I have solved a fair number of concrete problems. But I have rarely stepped back and turned any of that experience into something reusable across contexts. Synthesizing scattered work into a clean definition matters more and more in AI research, and it is exactly the skill that STEM training does not deliberately build.
