On Knowing Things: A Public Misjudgment

2026-07-29
TL;DR

The stock market taught me that most of what I believed had never been tested, because ordinary life never prices a wrong idea. The 2023 claim that China would never catch up in AI failed for the same reason: a static, single-variable model applied to an adaptive system. Knowing a thing takes three layers of method—On Practice for where knowledge comes from, Munger's mental models for checking it, and On Contradiction for reading where it is going.

An Unexamined Belief

For years I took something for granted: a person does not need to actively work on their own understanding. The assumption was rarely said out loud, but it ran my behavior. When a fact clashed with my judgment, my first reaction was that the fact was wrong, that someone had misread it, or that this case was special. I did not think my way of seeing the world had flaws, so fixing it never seemed worth dedicated time.

Trading stocks is what broke it. After entering the market I found that quite a few of my ideas about the world were simply wrong, and the errors came with a price: every bad call turned into a loss in my account within weeks. Once mistakes are priced, "my understanding is probably fine" is a sentence you can no longer say.

The experience raised a question: what attitude should a person take toward their own picture of the world? Watching people around me, two attitudes show up. The first assumes you are mostly right: I am a normal person, and the way I make sense of the world works. The second assumes you might be wrong. The difference becomes visible the moment a counterintuitive fact arrives. People in the first camp explain outward: the fault lies with other people, with the data, with this particular case. Only people in the second camp can explain inward: maybe my understanding does not apply here. For someone holding the first attitude, the system has no self-correction mechanism; no matter how strong the outside evidence, it gets explained away.

Why It Happened in the Stock Market

Why did the attitude change happen in the stock market and not somewhere else in life? I think the answer is the feedback mechanism. Most of life does not settle accounts for wrong ideas. A wrong worldview can ride along with a person for years, because nothing tests it on a schedule and nothing prices the error. Get politics wrong and nobody comes to collect. Get an industry trend wrong and other people pay most of the cost.

The stock market is a rare exception. A judgment settles into profit or loss within weeks, a wrong call means a loss, and the loss cannot be dodged. For the first time, my understanding had clear right-or-wrong feedback. The lesson I took from it: actively correcting your understanding requires first entering a practice environment that generates fast feedback. People without such an environment simply lack the trigger signals in daily life, and that is a different thing from refusing to correct.

A Public Misjudgment

Later I found that "assuming you are right" has a public version, and the public version has more visible consequences.

ChatGPT launched in December 2022. Through 2023, a set of judgments spread widely on the Chinese internet: Why can't China build a ChatGPT; China will never catch up with OpenAI; you cannot train a strong model on "404 data," a jab at the censored Chinese internet. These claims were stated with high confidence, long horizons, and almost no testable conditions attached.

Time did the testing. On July 16, 2026, Moonshot AI released Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts (MoE) model. On the composite intelligence index of Artificial Analysis, an independent evaluator, K3 ranked third globally, behind only Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol. It was the top open-weights model and ranked first on the frontend coding arena. Reservations exist: its general chat ranking sits well below its coding ranking, and its hallucination rate ticked back up versus the previous generation. But "can't catch up" is no longer a claim worth discussing.

An earlier test came in January 2025. On January 20, DeepSeek released R1, which tied OpenAI's reasoning model o1 on math and code benchmarks. The o1 preview shipped on September 12, 2024, and the full version on December 5, 2024. R1 arrived four months after the preview and six and a half weeks after the full release. The technical details matter more: R1's core reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm, GRPO, came from DeepSeek's DeepSeekMath paper of February 2024, seven months before o1 went public. A four-month time gap, plus a homegrown algorithm seven months ahead, means the company already held every technical piece it needed at the time. The real gap was about who dared to put major resources behind an unproven direction.

I had a position in this debate myself. In 2023 I was doing agent research on top of a locally deployed ChatGLM3, a 6B-parameter model. In use, it felt on par with the GPT-3.5 Turbo of the day. Two later leaks indirectly backed that impression: a Microsoft paper labeled GPT-3.5 Turbo as 20B parameters, and a USC study reverse-engineered it through API attacks at roughly the 7B scale. OpenAI never published a number, but "3.5 Turbo is a distilled small model" is a conclusion with fairly solid evidence. Based on this, I judged at the time that China needed two years to catch up with the US. It took three, from mid-2023 to mid-2026. I was off by a year. That error can be located: it sat in the parameter for the rate of catch-up, and it can be reviewed. That is a different kind of error from "will never catch up." This distinction is the core of the rest of this piece.

Where the 2023 Judgments Went Wrong

Take the 2023 batch of judgments apart and five concrete errors show up.

First, mistaking a time gap for a capability gap. The observation that China had no GPT-4-level model at the time was true. The error came in the next step: jumping from "doesn't have it now" to "won't have it later." In hindsight, the gap was a time delay that kept narrowing.

Second, underestimating the follower's cost advantage. Opening a technical route and reproducing a validated one differ in cost by an order of magnitude. OpenAI paid to explore the direction; followers pay only to reproduce it, on top of what the open-source ecosystem and published papers diffuse for free.

Third, overestimating how binding compute controls would be. There was stockpiling before the controls and compliant chips after them. More importantly, gains in algorithmic efficiency cut the compute needed for the same capability severalfold. DeepSeek V3, a frontier model trained on restricted compute, is the most direct counterexample to this misjudgment.

Fourth, confusing the source of training text with the ceiling on capability. Frontier Chinese models train mostly on English public web pages, books, code, and papers, a corpus shared globally. The quality of the Chinese internet affects performance in Chinese-language scenarios; it does not set the ceiling on reasoning and coding ability.

Fifth, extrapolating the future from the situation of the moment. The marvel of 2023 was ChatGPT the product, built on 2020 technology. By the time a real generational shift in capability arrived, the center of competition had moved to post-training and test-time compute. Both directions are heavy on engineering and light on capital moats. The conditions of technical competition were shifting in the catcher's favor.

The Common Pattern Behind the Errors

Behind the five errors sits one root: using a static, single-variable model to predict an adaptive, multi-variable system. The same pattern shows up again and again in judgments about the economy and about industry trends. I think it runs on four mechanisms.

First, judging by stock while outcomes are set by rates of change. What you could observe in 2023 was stock: the existing gap between models, the existing distribution of chips. The answer to a catch-up question lives in rates of change. Stock is visible; rates of change take calculation. People default to stock.

Second, assuming the other players in the system will not respond. Forecasters treat constraints as fixed: compute restricted today, restricted forever. In reality, constraints provoke responses; the controls pushed innovation in architectural efficiency. Nearly every "the moat cannot be crossed" claim quietly assumes the people on both sides of the moat do nothing.

Third, taking the most visible constraint for the tightest one. Compute and data were the most talked-about variables of 2023, because they can be counted, sanctioned, and turned into news. What actually constrained things was the speed of knowledge diffusion and the density of engineering talent. Those two variables are hard to quantify and rarely reported. Easy to measure is not the same as important.

Fourth, judgments generated by narrative carry no rule for their own revision. A narrative-born judgment does not come with an "I will change my mind if X appears" clause. Most of the people who said "will never catch up" in 2023 did not review their miss after DeepSeek's release in 2025; they moved straight to the next narrative. Here is the difference between a judgment and a narrative: when a judgment fails, you can locate which parameter failed; when a narrative fails, you just swap in a new narrative.

The four mechanisms compress into one sentence: mistaking a contradiction's temporary equilibrium under one set of conditions for a permanent state.

Three Questions in Knowing a Thing

The earlier sections were about where wrong judgments go wrong; this section gives the positive view. Knowing a thing is a process, and the process has three questions to answer in order. First, where does knowledge come from. Second, once you have it, how do you make sure it is right. Third, things develop, so how do you analyze the development. Each question has an established method behind it: On Practice, Munger's multiple mental models, and On Contradiction.

Question One: Where Knowledge Comes From

On Practice answers: knowledge comes from practice. Through practice a person gets direct experience of a thing, perceptual knowledge. Perceptual knowledge, abstracted and generalized, rises into rational knowledge: concepts, judgments, laws. Whether the rational knowledge is right can only be tested by putting it back into practice; if it fails the test, you revise and test again. It is a loop.

On Practice covers the two ends of this loop: the production of knowledge and its adjudication. It gives no concrete analysis tools, but it sets two disciplines. First, without direct contact with the thing, without investigation, you have no standing to judge it. Second, the final verdict on any piece of knowledge belongs to practice, not to authority, not to majority opinion, and not to a theory's internal consistency.

The experience in the earlier section belongs to this layer. Before trading, many of my beliefs had never been tested, because most everyday judgments produce no feedback. After trading, judgments settled into profit and loss within weeks, and my knowledge entered the loop of production, testing, and revision for the first time. The change in attitude was the result; the loop was the cause.

Question Two: How to Make Sure the Knowledge Is Right

The method at this layer is Charlie Munger's multiple mental models. A definition first. People understand things through models: a model is a compressed description of a class of regularities, abstracted from a discipline, such as supply and demand from economics, critical mass from physics, natural selection from biology. Every single model has two built-in defects. It has conditions of applicability and fails outside them. It also describes one facet of a thing while systematically ignoring the rest. Analyzing with a single model therefore produces systematic bias.

Multiple mental models correct for this: learn the foundational models from many disciplines, run the same object through several of them, and compare the results. When independent models agree, confidence in the conclusion rises. When they conflict, the point of conflict is where your understanding is most likely wrong, and you go back to layer one and resolve it through investigation and practice. Munger's estimate is that the important models across the major disciplines number eighty or ninety; a core subset covers most judgment situations.

The earlier analysis of the 2023 misjudgment used this layer. The people behind those claims were thinking inside one model: compute determinism. Checked against the latecomer-advantage model from economic history, the knowledge-diffusion model from innovation studies, and the engineering experience that constraints drive efficiency innovation, three independent models, all pointed the opposite way. Conflict this concentrated says the problem lies in applying compute determinism to this class of question at all.

Question Three: How to Analyze Development

The first two layers handle a thing's current state. But the object of knowledge itself develops; a description true today can fail tomorrow. On Contradiction provides the framework for analyzing development. Its basic claim: every thing contains opposing aspects within it, and the two sides of a contradiction both depend on and exclude each other. The balance of forces between the two sides determines the thing's current state. When conditions change, the balance changes with them; past a certain threshold, each side transforms into its opposite, and the thing shows a new state.

Analyzing a thing's development with this framework takes three steps. First, identify the principal contradiction currently governing the thing. Second, judge which side of the contradiction is dominant, since that fixes the present state. Third, find the conditions that could shift the balance, since that fixes the direction.

The framework also explains why rearview-mirror investing fails: it infers the future from a contradiction's past equilibrium, while returns depend on which side will dominate in the future, and between past and future, conditions have already changed the balance. On Contradiction adds that external causes are the conditions of change while internal causes are its basis, and external causes work through internal ones. The same external change acting on things with different internal structures produces different results. So judging direction takes more than watching external conditions; you have to analyze the thing's internal structure.

The 2023 misjudgment's error at this layer: it took the balance of forces at the time as fixed, never asked which conditions would change it, and attached no failure conditions to the judgment.

How the Three Layers Relate

The three questions come in order, and the three methods divide the work. Knowledge is produced by practice and judged by practice: layer one. The knowledge it produces gets organized as models, and multiple models cross-check each other to surface and correct errors: layer two. Things keep developing, and contradiction analysis extends knowledge of the current state into a judgment about direction: layer three. Layer one owns source and verdict, layer two owns organization and error correction, layer three owns extension and prediction. Together the three cover the full process of knowing a thing.
